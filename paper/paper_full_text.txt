Abstract
Tree-based classifiers like Random Forest and XGBoost are the standard choice for network intrusion detection. On clean traffic
from modern IoT benchmarks, they easily achieve over 99% accuracy. But what happens when an attacker modifies packet flows to
slip past the detector without breaking TCP connections? In this paper, we evaluate the robustness of Random Forest, XGBoost,
LightGBM, and a multi-layer perceptron on the CIC-IoT2023 dataset (701,978 flows, 31 attack classes). To keep the attacks
realistic, we build a multi-objective genetic algorithm that changes only continuous flow statistics—like packet size and inter-arrival
timing—while leaving discrete TCP flags, ports, and protocols intact.
Our experiments reveal three key findings. First, tree ensembles do not degrade gradually under perturbation; they drop off a cliff.
For XGBoost, evasion stays under 2.40% until the perturbation budget reaches ε= 4.69, where evasion suddenly spikes to 63.20%.
We trace this jump directly to the root decision split on flow packet count. Second, neural networks handle these tabular attacks
poorly: the MLP collapsed to 100% evasion, while XGBoost kept 44% recall under identical limits. Third, Random Forest shows an
interesting split: it repels attacks transferred from other models (only 2.00% transfer evasion), but fails almost completely when an
attacker can query it directly (99.00% evasion).
Standard adversarial retraining on decision trees usually causes overfitting, as trees build narrow leaves around synthetic attack
points and lose accuracy on normal traffic. To prevent this, we propose Gaussian Subspace Smoothing (GSS). GSS injects small
Gaussian noise into mutable features during training. This spreads discrete attack samples into continuous density clouds. This
forces the trees to learn broader, smoother split boundaries. Retrained with GSS, XGBoost’s evasion rate drops from 41.67% to
1.33%, with no loss in clean detection recall (99.33%). All code, datasets, and models are available in our public repository.

1. Introduction
When network security teams build automated intrusion
detection systems (NIDS), they almost always turn to deci-
sion tree ensembles like Random Forest, XGBoost, and Light-
GBM [1, 2, 3, 4]. On tabular network logs, these algorithms
are hard to beat: they train in seconds, handle messy feature
scales without extensive tuning, and regularly report benchmark
detection rates above 99% [5].
The catch is that high benchmark scores assume testing data
follows the same distribution as training logs [6]. In the real
world, attackers do not play by the rules of independent and
identically distributed (i.i.d.) data. Adversarial machine learn-
ing has repeatedly demonstrated that tiny, well-chosen feature
changes can blind a classifier while going unnoticed by security
analysts [7, 8, 9].
Most adversarial ML work focuses on image classification,
where an attacker can tweak pixel values freely within [0,1]

using gradient descent (e.g., FGSM [7] or PGD [8]). But net-
work traffic is fundamentally different [10, 11]. Flow records
mix continuous measurements (such as flow duration and byte
counts) with discrete counters and strict protocol flags [12, 13].
If an attacker naively edits categorical features—like setting
a TCP SYN flag to a decimal or making an inter-arrival time
negative—edge firewalls drop the packet immediately, or the
underlying TCP handshake breaks [11]. An evasion attack is
only dangerous if the resulting packets can actually travel across
a real network.
Tree ensembles present another practical roadblock: they
are non-differentiable. Because decision trees make decisions
using step functions, their loss gradients with respect to input
features are zero almost everywhere [14, 15]. Gradient-based
attacks get stuck at the start. To bypass tree models under
realistic conditions, we need zeroth-order optimization methods
that can explore discrete, constrained spaces without computing
gradients. Multi-objective genetic algorithms (GAs) fit this
problem naturally [16, 17].
Existing literature still leaves big gaps when it comes to tree-
based intrusion detection under realistic constraints. Many pa-
pers allow impossible feature edits and report inflated evasion

numbers that would never survive on an Ethernet wire. Other
studies test single models in isolation. As a result, comparing
tree architectures against deep neural networks on equal foot-
ing remains difficult. Finally, hardening tree models through
adversarial retraining has always been tricky: trees tend to build
narrow, overfitted leaves around synthetic attack samples. This
hurts performance on clean traffic without building genuine de-
fense [18].
In this paper, we address these challenges directly. We frame
our study around three specific questions:
1. RQ1 (Constrained Evasion): How easily can an attacker
evade tree-based NIDS when perturbations are strictly con-
fined to valid, continuous traffic statistics?
2. RQ2 (Decision Geometry): How does the geometry of
decision boundaries—orthogonal tree boxes versus smooth
neural manifolds—shape evasion success, confidence loss,
and transferability?
3. RQ3 (Defensive Trade-offs): Can we retrain tree ensem-
bles to resist adaptive attacks without hurting detection
accuracy on benign traffic?
To answer these questions, we conduct an empirical study
on the CIC-IoT2023 dataset [19]. Our experiments evaluate
701,978 flow records across 31 attack classes from 105 physical
IoT devices. Our main contributions are:
• Feature-Constrained Genetic Evasion: We build an evo-
lutionary evasion algorithm that restricts mutations strictly
to continuous statistical features selected by SHAP attri-
butions [20]. Discrete flags and protocol headers remain
untouched so that every generated flow remains physically
valid on the wire.
• Discovery of the Evasion Phase Transition: We show that
tree ensembles do not weaken gradually; they experience a
sharp phase transition at ε≈4.69, where XGBoost evasion
jumps from 2.40% to 63.20%. We trace this exact threshold
to the distance between malicious flows and root tree splits
on packet counts.
• Unmasking the Random Forest Paradox: We uncover a
sharp difference between black-box and white-box settings:
Random Forest repels surrogate transfer attacks almost en-
tirely (2.00% evasion), but folds under direct white-box
query access (99.00% evasion). This shows that bagging
provides security through obscurity rather than true mathe-
matical resilience.
• Architectural Comparison (Trees vs. Neural Nets): We
show that continuous neural decision surfaces (MLP) col-
lapse completely under constrained search (100.00% ASR,
zero residual recall), whereas XGBoost maintains 44.00%
recall. This confirms that orthogonal tree partitions provide
structural advantages on tabular data compared to continu-
ous neural manifolds.
• Gaussian Subspace Smoothing (GSS) Defense: We in-
troduce GSS, an adversarial retraining method that re-
places static attack points with continuous Gaussian clouds
across the mutable feature subspace. AT-XGBoost cuts
evasion from 41.67% to 1.33% while keeping clean re-
call at 99.33%, solving the traditional trade-off between
robustness and clean accuracy.
• Granular Per-Class Analysis and Open Artifacts: We
evaluate all 31 individual attack classes before and after
defense. Volumetric floods become virtually impenetrable
(0.00% evasion), whereas low-rate application exploits still
require payload inspection. All code, trained models, and
data scripts are publicly released on GitHub.

2. Related Work
Adversarial attacks on machine learning originated in com-
puter vision, but bringing those concepts into network security in-
troduces unique engineering challenges. This section reviews the
literature on adversarial intrusion detection, non-differentiable
tree ensembles, and heuristic search.
2.1. Foundations of Adversarial Machine Learning
Adversarial machine learning began with Szegedy et al. [21],
who showed that adding imperceptible noise to images could
mislead deep neural networks into confident misclassifications.
Goodfellow et al. [7] explained this using high-dimensional lin-
earity and introduced the one-step FGSM attack. Madry et al. [8]
later formalized adversarial defense as a minimax game and es-
tablished multi-step PGD as the standard first-order benchmark.
Carlini and Wagner [9] cautioned that many proposed defenses
merely masked gradients rather than securing models, while
Croce and Hein [22] developed AutoAttack to prevent selective
evaluation reporting.
2.2. Adversarial Vulnerabilities in Network Intrusion Detection
Bringing these ideas into network security is not straightfor-
ward [10]. As Sommer and Paxson pointed out in their classic
paper [6], intrusion detection operates in an open world with
huge class imbalance, high false-positive penalties, and strict
protocol rules. Early adversarial NIDS studies often changed tab-
ular values arbitrarily in feature space [17]. Pierazzi et al. [12]
clarified why this is problematic: an attacker operates in the
problem space (real bytes and packets), and changes must pro-
duce valid software or traffic. Apruzzese et al. [11] showed that
ignoring transport semantics leads to wildly exaggerated attack
success, since flipped TCP flags cause socket drops. Sheatsley
et al. [13] confirmed that enforcing protocol constraints signifi-
cantly changes how attacks unfold. In our work, we respect these
boundaries by locking discrete flags and restricting perturbations
to continuous flow statistics.

2.3. The Challenge of Attacking and Hardening Tree Ensembles
Most adversarial research focuses on neural networks, but
tree ensembles—Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM—
dominate enterprise tabular data due to their speed, low resource
usage, and tabular accuracy [1, 2, 3]. Evaluating their robust-
ness mathematically is difficult: trees partition feature space into
piecewise-constant orthogonal boxes, and their loss gradients
are zero almost everywhere [14]. Kantchelian et al. [14] used
Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) to find minimal
adversarial perturbations for trees. However, MILP scales ex-
ponentially with tree depth and ensemble size, which makes it
impractical for production ensembles with hundreds of trees.
Chen et al. [15] examined robust splitting approximations, and
Calzavara et al. [23] introduced Treant to build evasion-aware de-
cision trees. However, empirical studies testing modern boosting
algorithms against semantically constrained attacks on modern
IoT traffic remain scarce.
2.4. Evolutionary Search and Adversarial Defenses
Because discrete network rules and non-differentiable tree
splits prevent gradient calculations, evolutionary computation
offers a practical alternative [16]. Deb et al.’s NSGA-II [16]
established multi-objective optimization under competing goals.
In security, genetic algorithms have been used to generate eva-
sive PDF files and malware binaries where gradients do not
exist [17]. For NIDS, GAs allow us to balance evasion confi-
dence against perturbation budgets while enforcing hard masks
over protocol fields.
On the defensive side, randomized smoothing by Cohen et
al. [24] guarantees certified ℓ2 bounds for neural networks by
sampling isotropic Gaussian noise at test time, but the required
hundreds of Monte Carlo passes per flow are too slow for line-
rate packet monitoring. Adversarial retraining remains the most
practical empirical defense [8]. Yet for decision trees, standard
data augmentation often fails because trees isolate synthetic
attack points into deep, overfitted leaves. This degrades clean
accuracy without establishing a genuinely robust boundary [18].
Taking inspiration from tabular interpolation techniques like
SMOTE [25], our Gaussian Subspace Smoothing (GSS) method
injects continuous noise into mutable features during training.
This prevents trees from memorising individual coordinates.

of the art, Table 1 compares representative literature against our

3. Methodology and Threat Model
Evaluating evasion under realistic protocol constraints re-
quires tying feature edits to physical network mechanics. Below,
we formalize our dataset pipeline, threat model, evolutionary
search algorithm, and the Gaussian Subspace Smoothing de-
fense. Figure 1 presents the architectural overview of the full
research pipeline.
3.1. Benchmark Dataset and Preprocessing
To test detection resilience on real hardware, we use the CIC-
IoT2023 dataset [19] from the Canadian Institute for Cybersecu-
rity. The data comes from a physical testbed of 105 IoT devices
that includes IP cameras, home automation sensors, and micro-
controllers. The capture covers 33 attack scenarios alongside
normal background traffic.
The raw PCAP data includes 40 statistical flow features ex-
tracted from forward and backward packet directions. We in-
gested the data using Parquet files and applied a clean prepro-
cessing routine:
1. Dropping Invalids: We removed records with infinite or
missing values caused by zero-division in flow calculations
(e.g., variance on flows with a single packet).
2. Binary and Multi-Class Labels: The raw dataset contains
31 attack classes plus benign traffic. In production SOCs,
detectors first flag flows as benign or malicious before rout-
ing them to deeper triage. We mapped labels to binary
targets (y = 0 for Benign, y = 1 for Malicious) while keep-
ing original multi-class labels for per-attack vulnerability
breakdowns.
3. Removing Invariant Columns: We pruned constant
columns and static identifiers, which leaves an active fea-
ture space of d= 39 dimensions.
4. Stratified Train/Test Split: We split the dataset into an
80% training partition (Ntrain = 561,569) and a 20% test
partition (Ntest = 140,393) using stratified sampling. Fea-
tures were standardized using z-scores (z = (x−µ)/σ),
where µ and σ were computed strictly from the training
set to prevent data leakage. The dataset exhibits a realistic
41.96:1 attack-to-benign imbalance ratio. Table 2 summa-
rizes the data splits.

3.2.3. Attacker’s Constraints
Unlike naive feature-space attacks that alter arbitrary numbers,
we enforce strict domain constraints:
1. Locking Discrete Flags: The attacker cannot mod-
ify categorical or protocol-mandated fields (e.g., TCP
SYN/FIN/RST flags, protocol IDs, port numbers). Flip-
ping these fields causes transport resets, breaks handshakes,
or gets dropped by network hardware [11].
2. Restricting to Mutable Features: Perturbations are con-
fined strictly to a verified subset of mutable continuous fea-
tures Fmut ⊂{1,...,d}. For any invariant feature j Fmut,
we require δj = 0.
3.3. Finding Mutable Features with SHAP
To choose which features belong in Fmut, we combined fea-
ture variance checks with TreeSHAP [20]. We first filtered for
continuous features with more than 15 unique values in the test
set. Next, we ran TreeSHAP on a 1,000-sample slice of the
baseline XGBoost classifier.
Figure 2 shows the SHAP beeswarm plot. The result high-
lights a glaring vulnerability: the single feature Number (packet
count per flow) accounts for 92.25% of the model’s total deci-
sion weight. The remaining 7.75% is spread thinly across all
other features.
Why does this happen? In IoT environments, normal traffic
(MQTT telemetry, NTP pings, DNS requests) typically consists
of small flows with only 2 to 10 packets. In contrast, volumetric
attacks (UDP floods, SYN floods, Mirai scans) blast thousands
of packets within seconds. Decision tree algorithms quickly find
that splitting on packet count yields immediate Gini impurity
drops, so they place Number at the root of almost every tree.

While this yields 99% clean accuracy, it creates a dangerous
shortcut. An attacker does not need to solve a complex opti-
mization problem; they only need to pad flow durations or pace
transmissions to push packet counts past the tree’s split point.
Based on this ranking, we selected the top 15 continuous features
as Fmut, including Duration, Tot size of bckwd packet, Tot size
of fwd packet, Fwd Packet Length Max, and inter-arrival times
(Flow IAT Mean, Bckwd IAT Mean). These correspond to packet
padding, jitter injection, and transmission pacing, which an at-
tacker can implement in software without breaking application
payloads.

icated tabular smoothing, deep tabular neural classifiers
present severe operational risk.
2. Deploy Adversarially Trained XGBoost with Subspace
Smoothing: AT-XGBoost with 20% GSS augmentation
establishes the current state of the art in robust tabular
NIDS, suppressing ASR to 1.33% with zero penalty on
clean detection recall.
3. Account for the Random Forest Security Paradox: Do
not mistake bagging for intrinsic robustness. Random For-

cause its split angles were decorrelated from XGBoost, not
because its boundaries were sturdy. Direct query access
breached it easily (99.00% ASR).

9.4. Study Limitations
We acknowledge several operational constraints:
• Feature Space vs. Problem Space Execution: While our
attack restricts perturbations to semantically verified con-
tinuous flow features, it operates on pre-extracted statistical
summaries rather than injecting raw packet bytes into live
Ethernet interfaces. Minor discrepancies may arise when
translating continuous statistical targets into discrete packet
padding.
• Static Benchmark Scope: Evaluations were conducted
on the CIC-IoT2023 corpus. While CIC-IoT2023 provides
modern, high-fidelity traffic across 105 devices, validation
across multi-enterprise enterprise benchmarks (e.g., CIC-
IDS2018, UNSW-NB15) will provide further confirmation
of generalizability.
• Offline Training Assumptions: Our defense assumes pe-
riodic batch retraining. In streaming production networks
subject to zero-day concept drift, online adaptive retraining
mechanisms will be necessary.

10. Conclusion and Future Work
Tree ensembles remain the practical standard for network
flow inspection, but high clean accuracy conceals structural
vulnerabilities to feature-constrained evasion. In this work, we
probed Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and a deep MLP
on 701,978 flow records from the CIC-IoT2023 dataset under
semantically constrained evolutionary attacks.
Four core insights emerge:
1. Tree-based models display an abrupt, non-linear phase tran-
sition in evasion vulnerability at ε≈4.69, where XGBoost
ASR jumps from 2.40% to 63.20% as perturbations cross
primary split thresholds on packet counts.
2. Deep Multi-Layer Perceptrons exhibit extreme adversarial
brittleness, suffering 100.00% ASR under identical con-
straints that XGBoost resists with a 43.00% margin. This
confirms that axis-aligned orthogonal boundaries offer dis-
tinct structural advantages over continuous neural mani-
folds on tabular data.
3. Random Forest exhibits an unexpected security paradox:
high black-box transfer resistance (2.00% ASR) paired with
near-complete white-box collapse (99.00% ASR).
4. Adversarial training augmented with Gaussian Subspace
Smoothing (GSS) resolves the historical conflict between
robustness and utility in tree ensembles: it cuts XGBoost
evasion from 41.67% to 1.33% while maintaining 99.33%
clean detection recall.
Looking forward, we see four promising directions: testing
packet-level perturbations directly in live hardware testbeds via
DPDK, developing dynamic thresholding against streaming con-
cept drift, exploring robustness in federated IoT deployments,
and establishing certified robustness guarantees for tree ensem-
bles through inference-time subspace smoothing.
